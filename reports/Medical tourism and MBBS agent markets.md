# Broken trust opens two India–Kazakhstan businesses

Both projects are worth building for the same reason: in each corridor, the intermediaries are paid by the supplier, checked by nobody, and missing when things go wrong, and nobody sells accountability to the customer. India already receives **13,699 Uzbek and 10,231 Turkmen medical travellers a year** (2025), but it holds only an estimated 5–10% of Kazakhstan's **~$0.95 billion outbound medical market**, which Korea and Turkey dominate. The patients who do come are served by hospital desks that each sell one hospital, by Bookimed's commission-funded marketplace, and by a long tail of agents with no reviews. Documented cases from 2023–2026 show families losing money and lives in the non-clinical layer around good hospitals: a Kazakh teenager died in India before treatment began, after his family paid an intermediary 15–20 million tenge; a Kyrgyz transplant family died in a guesthouse fire beside Max Saket; an Uzbek patient was robbed of $16,000 in cash. The profitable opening for a Russian-speaking doctor licensed in both countries is a **fixed-fee, hospital-neutral patient-advocate service launched from Kazakhstan**. It must be fixed-fee, because India's ethics code bars doctors from taking referral commissions "directly or indirectly", and an NMC notice of 6 October 2026 reportedly adds lead-generation fees. That turns the industry-standard 20–35% hospital commission into a licence risk for this founder in particular. For the MBBS app, about **25,000 Indian students a year** go abroad through a university–contractor–sub-agent chain that Indian embassies openly distrust; FMGE pass rates fell to about **12% in June 2026**, and Kyrgyzstan's accreditation purge left **2,000+ Indian students** in limbo, yet nobody runs a verified, neutral agent marketplace. The app solves a real problem only if verification, not lead volume, is the product. On realistic assumptions it is a **₹2–25 crore-a-year business**, not a venture-scale one, and its pay-per-contact mechanism must be rebuilt around India's DPDP consent and child-data rules, which take full effect on 14 May 2027.

Single-source figures are flagged as such. Counsel must check every legal conclusion here against the primary text before anyone relies on it. That applies above all to the NMC notice of 6 October 2026, Kazakh Order ҚР ДСМ-105/2020, Uzbek data law Art. 27-1 and the DPDP commencement schedule.

## Project 1: Central Asian patients reach India through middlemen nobody vouches for

### India already wins Uzbeks and Turkmens but barely competes for Kazakhs

India recorded **507,244 medical-purpose foreign arrivals in 2025**. Two Central Asian countries are in its top five sources, behind only Bangladesh and Iraq: **Uzbekistan is third with 13,699 and Turkmenistan fifth with 10,231** ([PIB](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/may/doc202652862301.pdf)). In 2024, when Bangladeshi demand collapsed and India's medical visas fell 22% to 4.64 lakh, both were among the few sources whose visas *grew* ([Factly](https://factly.in/data-medical-visas-issued-by-india-down-more-than-20-in-2024-on-account-of-political-unrest-in-bangladesh/)). Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Russia are not in the top seven, and no official Indian count exists for any of them. So the Uzbek and Turkmen flows already lean towards India, while the others are still contestable.

Kazakhstan is the biggest and richest prize, and India is losing it. The only full-market study, by Ulagat Consulting Group, counted **just under 114,000 Kazakh medical travellers spending $948.1 million in 2023**, mostly for cancer treatment and to clarify or confirm a diagnosis ([bes.media](https://bes.media/news/na-lechenie-za-rubezhom-kazahstantsi-potratili-948-mln-dollarov-v-2023-godu-issledovanie-9d568c/)). The national medical tourism association puts the number lower, at 40,000–60,000 a year ([MedMost](https://medmost.org/treatment-of-kazakhstanis-abroad/)). A defensible planning range is 50,000–115,000 trips worth $0.5–1.0 billion, or roughly $8,300 per traveller on Ulagat's figures.

Korea took **about 14,000 Kazakh patients in 2024, up 22.6%**, mainly for health check-ups and dermatology ([Korea MOHW](https://www.mohw.go.kr/board.es?mid=a10503010100&bid=0027&act=view&list_no=1485191&tag=&nPage=2)). Kazakhs were the **highest per-patient spenders among all foreign patients in Korea, at KRW 6.08 million** each ([Financial News](https://www.fnnews.com/news/202609150925082722)). Turkey takes another 12,000–15,000 a year. India's share is an unofficial, unsourced "up to 6,000" ([MedMost](https://medmost.org/treatment-of-kazakhstanis-abroad/)), roughly 5–10% of the market. Germany and Israel hold the premium oncology end.

The rest of the region looks different. Uzbek border statistics recorded **15,200 outbound "treatment" trips in January–April 2025 alone** ([Upl.uz](https://upl.uz/obshestvo/51540-news.html)). That is likely an undercount, since many patients declare tourism or family visits. Kyrgyz patients travel mainly for transplants, to India, Turkey, China and Pakistan. One Kyrgyz outlet quotes transplants "from $50,000" in India against "from $85,000" in Turkey, and kidney transplants in India "from $18,000" ([Vecherny Bishkek](https://www.vb.kg/doc/304490_medicinskiy_tyrizm:_kyda_i_zachem_kyrgyzstancy_edyt_lechitsia.html)). The stakes are high: a Kyrgyz official says **20–25 citizens die each year in Turkey and India** while abroad for treatment ([Vecherny Bishkek](https://www.vb.kg/doc/459209_s_nachala_goda_v_kyrgyzstan_dostavili_tela_174_grajdan_ymershih_za_rybejom.html)).

Tajik patients mostly go to Uzbekistan, which received **86,199 foreign patients in 2025, 59,145 of them Tajik** ([NUZ](https://nuz.uz/2026/01/27/v-uzbekistan-v-2025-godu-dlya-lecheniya-pribyli-bolee-86-tysyach-inostranczev/)). Kazakhstan reports about 80,000 inbound medical tourists in 2024 ([Times of Central Asia](https://timesca.com/kazakhstan-emerges-as-regional-leader-in-medical-tourism/)). These regional hubs take the lower-acuity, budget cases, and Korea owns check-ups and aesthetics. India's defensible niche is therefore **high-acuity, price-sensitive care**: oncology, related-donor liver and kidney transplants, cardiac surgery, orthopaedics and paediatric bone-marrow transplants. Russians are a weaker target. Their top destinations in 2026 are Turkey, Belarus and Thailand, and India features mainly for Ayurveda and recovery programmes ([Inc. Russia](https://incrussia.ru/news/meditsinskij-turizm-v-2026-m-novoe-napravlenie-kuda-rossiyane-edut-ne-otdyhat/)).

Logistics favour India more than its market share suggests. Tashkent has about **13 nonstop Delhi flights a week** across four carriers ([FlightConnections](https://www.flightconnections.com/flights-from-tas-to-del)). Almaty–Delhi takes about **3.5 hours** ([Trip.com](https://www.trip.com/hot/almaty-to-delhi-flight-time/)), less than flying to Seoul. India has restored e-Medical and e-Medical Attendant visas for Uzbek and Kyrgyz nationals ([Embassy of India Tashkent](https://eoitashkent.gov.in/e-visa-scheme-for-uzbek-nationals/); [Embassy of India Bishkek](https://indembbishkek.gov.in/press-detail.php?id=19)); eligibility for Kazakhs, Tajiks and Turkmens was confirmed only on unofficial sites. Language splits the market: **over 90% of Kazakhstanis know Russian, against roughly half of Uzbeks** ([Times of Central Asia](https://timesca.com/uzbekistan-ratifies-agreement-to-establish-cis-russian-language-organization/)). A Russian-speaking founder covers Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan natively, but needs Uzbek-speaking staff to reach India's largest Central Asian source.

Kazakhstan's state-funded route abroad is small. **In 2025 it sent 70 patients to foreign clinics, at a cost of over 1 billion tenge** ([Turan Times](https://turantimes.kz/obschestvo/65658-kazahstancev-mogut-napravit-na-lechenie-za-rubezh-kto-mozhet-pretendovat-na-pomosch.html)). In 2024 India was one of the destinations, alongside Turkey, Germany, Russia and Belarus, at an average of about 13 million tenge per case ([CMN.kz](https://cmn.kz/lechenie-grazhdan-za-rubezhom/)), and seven Indian specialty hospitals sit on the approved list ([SEPC](https://www.servicesepc.org/upload/media/circular/Promotion%20of%20Indian%20Medical%20Tourism%20in%20Kazakhstan_2458.pdf)). Patients apply through health authorities, not intermediaries ([eGov.kz](https://egov.kz/cms/ru/services/health_care/pass-132-5_mz)), and the state chooses the clinic. The whole channel is worth roughly $1.5–2.5 million a year, so it matters for credibility, not volume.

### Six kinds of player already move these patients, and none answers to the family

| Player type | Named examples | Regional footprint | Who pays them | Core weakness for families |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hospitals selling direct | Max, Fortis, Medanta, Apollo (through local licensee UMT/Medicon), Aakash (owns a Tashkent hospital), Yashoda (Turkistan city agreement, Sept 2026), Lilavati (planned Kazakh outpatient centre) | Dense in Tashkent; thin in Kazakhstan | Hospital budget | Each recommends only its own hospital |
| Global marketplaces | Bookimed (Russian and Kazakh sites, Kazakh phone line), MediGlobus | Online | Clinic commission, rate not published | Provisional prices; complaints of steering; can send Kazakhs to Turkey or Korea instead |
| India-based facilitators | Vaidam, Lyfboat, Healthtrip, Regimen, Medikaya, Shifam, Ginger | Online; Vaidam's Tashkent presence unclear | Hospital commission | No physician or office in the patient's country; copy-paste content |
| Local agencies | medturist.kz, touradviser.kz, IndiaMed.kz, Avanta and ConsultIndia (Tashkent), MedStandard (Almaty, Bishkek), Easymedcare and MedExpress (Russia) | Local offices | Presumed hospital commission | Almost no reviews, no scale, no accountability |
| Informal brokers | Hospital interpreters who became brokers; translators from the patients' own countries | Delhi and Gurugram hospital districts | Commissions and tips | Unregulated and conflicted, yet often the only real support |
| Rival destinations | Korea (KHIDI office in Almaty), Turkey (Acıbadem, USHAŞ), Germany/Israel, Uzbek and Kazakh hubs | Government offices, roadshows, direct flights | State and hospital budgets | Turkey costs about 70% more for transplants; Korea is weak in complex surgery |

The most serious competitors are Indian hospitals, because they are going direct. Max runs a Tashkent patient-assistance centre with virtual consultations, interpreters and follow-up care ([Max Healthcare](https://www.maxhealthcare.in/international/en/center/max-healthcare-patient-assistance-center-uzbekistan)). Fortis lists information centres in Tashkent and Andijan ([Fortis](https://www.fortishealthcare.com/international-patients)). Apollo's Uzbek centre is run by a local licensee, UMT/Medicon ([apollo.uz](http://apollo.uz/)), and an Apollo-branded medical-tourism centre is listed in Almaty ([MedElement](https://medelement.com/companies/clinics/222497261732710857)). Aakash bought and reopened a Tashkent hospital in November 2022 ([IMTJ](https://www.laingbuissonnews.com/imtj/news-imtj/uzbekistan-aakash-health-buys-tashkent-hospital/)).

The push into Kazakhstan is recent. **In September 2026 Yashoda Medicity signed a three-year referral MoU with Turkistan city**, covering visas, appointments and admissions ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/health/hospital-diagnostics/yashoda-medicity-signs-mou-with-kazakhstans-turkistan-city-for-healthcare-cooperation-178379)). Lilavati plans a super-speciality outpatient centre in the country ([Indian Pharma Post](https://www.indianpharmapost.com/news/lilavati-hospital-to-launch-super-speciality-opd-in-kazakhstan-21324)).

Each hospital desk sells one hospital by design, and Max's follow-up covers only Max patients. Families have nobody neutral to compare offers. That is where the founder's contacts across several international desks become an asset rather than a conflict, provided none of those hospitals pays him.

Bookimed is the benchmark platform. It was founded in Kyiv in 2014 and has one reported funding round, a $500,000 seed. It reported **$22.1 million of 2025 booking value across 1,700 clinics** ([citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/?p=875900)), though that figure comes from a single press-release-style source. It names Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan among its main client countries ([Bookimed](https://us-uk.bookimed.com/team/)). It is free to patients and charges clinics a commission at an undisclosed rate. Its prices are explicitly provisional: materials, tests and extra nights are billed separately ([Bookimed finance policy](https://us-uk.bookimed.com/page/finance-policy/)). It has about 975 Trustpilot reviews averaging 4.5 stars ([Trustpilot](https://uk.trustpilot.com/review/www.bookimed.com?page=10)). Russian-language reviewers accuse it of steering patients to partner clinics and quoting about 1.5 times what they found elsewhere, although many of those reviews date from 2018–19 ([Otzovik](https://otzovik.com/reviews/ru_bookimed_com-mezhdunarodniy_centr_podderzhki_pacientov/)).

Bookimed's multi-country catalogue is a strength for Bookimed and a weakness for India, because a Kazakh lead can just as easily be sent to Istanbul or Seoul. If India makes up 10–20% of its booking value (a guess), Bookimed's India business is only a few million dollars. No well-funded player owns the CIS-to-India niche.

Facilitators based in India are small and lightly funded. Vaidam raised about $74,000 in seed money ([Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/companies/vaidam/__z17Gcw7_NypRTjiDdJbVJD790dfmVcYTVDdFp4YZkWM)) yet names Uzbekistan as a key source market ([IndiaMedToday](https://indiamedtoday.com/vaidam-healths-vision-for-medical-tourism-boom/)). Healthtrip raised a $5 million Series A ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/hospals)). Lyfboat is unusual in holding NABH accreditation as a medical-travel facilitator ([ITIJ](https://www.itij.com/latest/news/lyfboat-achieves-nabh-accreditation)).

A newer wave of SEO-driven firms publishes near-identical guides for patients from Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan. Medikaya claims Russian-speaking coordinators sit in most top-tier hospitals ([Medikaya](https://medikayahealthcare.com/medical-tourism-from-kazakhstan-to-india-step-by-step-complete-guide-2026/)); only Ginger claims to have Uzbek interpreters ([Ginger](https://ginger.healthcare/destinations/india/from/uzbekistan/)). On the ground, local agencies such as medturist.kz offer clinic selection, visas, flights, interpreters and translation of medical records ([Medturist.kz](https://medturist.kz/)). The visible ones carry almost no independent reviews; Tashkent's Avanta has none ([Top.uz](https://top.uz/company/avanta-lechenie-v-indii)).

Beneath all of this sits the informal layer that actually holds families together. Delhi's private hospitals have hired Russian-speaking Indian graduates as interpreters since about 2009, and some of them went on to become independent brokers ([Muth & Suryanarayan](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/140721/2/Muth_Suryanarayan_2020.pdf)). After the June 2026 guesthouse fire, it was translators, many of them migrants from Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan or Afghanistan, who found shelter for displaced patients, "not hospitals or medical-tourism companies" ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/ground-reports/india-medical-tourism-medical-translators-delhi-hauz-rani-fire/2994214/)). Turkmenistan is India's fifth-largest source of patients but has no visible agencies, which suggests that channel runs on word of mouth and these diaspora fixers.

Rival destinations recruit more systematically than India. Korea's KHIDI runs an Almaty office covering the whole CIS ([KHIDI](https://www.khidi.or.kr/eps)). Busan held B2B meetings in Almaty on 8–9 September 2026 to mark a new direct flight, and Central Asian patients in Korea rose 12.5% to 21,500 in 2025 ([Financial News](https://www.fnnews.com/news/202609150925082722)). Turkey received 1.5 million health tourists and over $3 billion in 2024 ([UNIT İstanbul](https://unitistanbul.com/turkiyede-saglik-turizmi-2024-verileri-ve-2025-2026-beklentileri/)), and Acıbadem has had a Tashkent representative office since 2014 ([Acıbadem](https://acibadem.com.ru/news/representative-office-uzbekistan/)). India's counter is price and flight time.

Across all six player types, the package is the same: a free quote, a visa invitation letter, airport pickup, an interpreter and accommodation, all paid for by hospital commission. Almost nobody offers binding all-inclusive prices, payment protection beyond Bookimed's refundable deposit, published outcomes, a licensed physician in the patient's home country, or structured follow-up after the patient returns home.

### Families lose money and lives in the non-clinical layer

The evidence splits cleanly. Patients praise Indian clinical quality, and complain about intermediaries, money and logistics. One Kazakh family reported almost no problems with the doctors: "our biggest difficulties were financial", after treatment cost 20 million tenge funded by loans ([bes.media](https://bes.media/news/besplatno-no-pozdno-pochemu-kazahstantsi-sobirayut-dengi-na-lechenie-raka/)).

**Quotes do not survive arrival.** In 2023 a senior Indian transplant surgeon told Kun.uz that many Uzbek patients had been deceived: prices rose or upfront payment was demanded after arrival, the go-between then became unreachable, and "package" prices mixed included and excluded services in ways patients did not understand ([Kun.uz](https://kun.uz/news/2023/10/25/hind-shifokori-hindistonda-davolanishni-istagan-bemorlarga-tavsiyalar-berdi)). This attribution comes from a search summary, so verify it before quoting the surgeon by name. In an older 2019 case, a Tashkent family expected to pay about $1,000 and was asked for $1,500–5,000 a day; the mother died, and the intermediaries disputed the family's account ([EADaily](https://eadaily.com/ru/news/2019/06/12/bolshe-nichego-ne-bolit-grazhdanka-uzbekistana-umerla-na-lechenii-v-indii)). In May 2026 a **16-year-old from Kyzylorda died on his 12th day in India before therapy began**. The reported cause was a vascular blockage, not the tumour. His mother says the family gave a Shymkent intermediary 15–20 million tenge and that the organisers "were simply pumping money out of us"; the company denies the accusation ([Zakon.kz](https://www.zakon.kz/mir/6517102-s-nas-prosto-vykachivali-dengi-podrostok-iz-kyzylordy-umer-v-indii-ne-dozhdavshis-lecheniya.html)). Fortis Gurugram reportedly refused to discharge a 14-year-old Kazakh patient over about $12,000 of unpaid bills ([Tengrinews](https://tengrinews.kz/kazakhstan_news/posolstvo-kazaxstana-pomozet-pogasit-dolg-14-letnego-252091/)), even though the Delhi High Court has held that unpaid bills cannot justify withholding a patient's release ([Bar and Bench](https://www.barandbench.com/columns/can-hospitals-hold-patients-hostage-over-unpaid-bills)). Families do not know they have that right, and nobody on their side is there to tell them.

**The commission model rewards misleading patients.** A hospital-marketing consultancy says Indian hospitals pay facilitators **20–35% of total treatment value** ([iChelon](https://ichelonconsulting.com/insights/medical-tourism-marketing-india-hospitals-2026)); a referral network puts the range at 7.5–30% ([My1Health](https://my1health.com/referral-partner-network/articles/steps-to-partnering-with-international-hospitals-for-medical-tourism)). Both are vendor sources. A pricing consultant alleges that hospitals inflate the quotes they give facilitators to cover that commission, so a $25,000 procedure becomes $30,000–35,000 ([Maria Todd](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/real-truth-medical-tourism-prices-abroad-maria-k-todd-mha-phd)). In Korea, a ministry official warned that brokers demand 30–70% of fees ([THE AsiaN](https://theasian.asia/archives/46325)).

The patient experience reflects this. Reviewers describe paying for a professor and seeing "an ordinary doctor", with coordination and interpreting "of very doubtful quality" ([Hospitals-travel.ru](https://hospitals-travel.ru/countries/india)). A study of online patient narratives tied complaints to "inaccurate web-based advertisements of the doctor's capabilities" ([JMIR](https://doi.org/10.2196/15665)). Families often choose a destination because a clinic offered a free consultation with a professor ([bes.media](https://bes.media/news/besplatno-no-pozdno-pochemu-kazahstantsi-sobirayut-dengi-na-lechenie-raka/)). In this market, a second opinion is a sales hook, not independent advice.

**Housing, cash and payments put families in physical danger.** On **3 June 2026, a fire at a guesthouse next to Max Super Speciality Hospital, Saket, killed 21 people** ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/21-killed-26-injured-in-delhi-hotel-fire-near-malviya-nagar-hospitals)). The guesthouse was in Hauz Rani, an urban village exempt from standard building bylaws, where high treatment costs push patients into cheap housing ([Scroll](https://amp.scroll.in/article/1093358/delhi-fire-deaths-highlight-precariousness-of-medical-tourists-in-the-city)). Among the dead were a Kyrgyz liver-transplant patient from Osh, her 23-year-old son, who had been her donor, and her mother, who had come to care for them; treatment was finished, and they were days from flying home ([24.kg](https://24.kg/obschestvo/376495_semya_priehala_nalechenie_chto_izvestno_opogibshih_vdeli_kyirgyizstantsah/)). Cash is the other hazard. An Uzbek kidney-transplant patient at Artemis was **robbed of $16,000 in cash by men posing as police in Gurugram** ([Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/haryana/in-town-for-treatment-uzbek-national-robbed-of-16000-by-fake-cops-in-gurugram-2824796)). Russians are pushed towards cash because cards issued in Russia generally do not work in India and transfers from Russia face heavy sanctions screening ([vc.ru](https://vc.ru/opinions/2935954-kak-perevesti-dengi-v-indiyu-iz-rossii)); accounts also conflict on whether Indian clinics stopped dealing directly with Russian assistance insurers in 2026 ([RBC](https://www.rbc.ru/society/21/02/2026/6999d9ed9a7947ab3aaf27ea)).

**Interpreters and transplant paperwork both fail families.** An integrative review found language barriers to be the most common problem medical tourists face ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7285947/)), yet the people who fix that problem are either paid by the hospital or earning commission as brokers.

Transplants, a core Central Asian segment, carry the heaviest paperwork and the highest legal stakes. A foreign recipient needs Authorisation Committee approval and an embassy-certified Form 21 confirming the relationship with the donor. An Indian donor is not allowed for a foreign recipient unless they are near relatives ([Lok Sabha AU1351](https://eparlib.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/2974437/1/AU1351.pdf)). A 2024 Delhi–Noida racket used forged Bangladeshi high-commission papers ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/health/doctors/bangladesh-india-kidney-transplant-racket-indraprastha-apollo-surgeon-held-for-running-racket-at-yatharth-hospital-131358)), and foreign patients now wait longer for approval ([Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/karnataka/foreigners-wait-longer-kidney-transplant-2144608)). Kazakhs have even received scam emails from a supposed "Indian clinic" offering $100,000–300,000 for a kidney ([Kazinform](https://www.inform.kz/ru/indiyskaya-klinika-hochet-kupit-pochki-kazahstancev-obzor-respublikanskoy-pressy-za-31-yanvarya_a2626462)).

**After return, almost nothing is verified.** Kazakh agencies advertise remote monitoring and delivery of "original Indian medicines" ([IndiaMed.kz](https://indiamed.kz/)), but no independent evidence shows this follow-up happens. In Uzbekistan, contaminated Indian cough syrup was linked to the deaths of 65 children ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/industry/pharma/story/uzbekistan-says-contaminated-indian-cough-syrup-that-killed-65-children-was-on-market-due-to-bribery-report-394428-2023-08-17)). There, mapping Indian brand-name drugs to their generic names and to locally registered equivalents is a real need, not a nicety.

Relatives carry the financial risk through loans and crowdfunding, travel as donors and carers, and depend on the intermediary they later accuse. Kazakhstan's Ministry of Health warned in August 2026 against guaranteed-cure promises and pressure to decide fast. It told patients to check accreditation, doctor qualifications and price transparency ([Kursiv](https://kz.kursiv.media/2026-08-11/lsbs-kak-vybrat-kliniku-za-rubezhom-i-ne-popast-k-moshennikam-sovety-minzdrava/)). That checklist is effectively a product specification for a new entrant.

|---|---|---|---|

### Hospital commissions are the one revenue line this founder cannot safely take

India's binding ethics code for doctors is still the 2002 Indian Medical Council regulations. The NMC's 2023 replacement was suspended in August 2023 ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/health-news/nmc/breaking-news-nmc-puts-its-controversial-registered-medical-practitioner-professional-conduct-regulations-2023-put-on-hold-116314)). **Clause 6.4 bars a registered doctor from receiving any commission or bonus for referring a patient, and from taking part "directly or indirectly" in splitting or rebating fees** ([Conventus Law](https://conventuslaw.com/report/rx-for-referrals-navigating-the-ethical-considerations-in-indias-medical-landscape/)).

On 6 October 2026 the NMC's Ethics and Medical Registration Board reportedly issued an advertising and communication notice, effective immediately ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/health-news/nmc/nmc-issues-ethical-advertising-public-communication-guidelines-for-doctors-hospitals-details-180731)). As reported, it bans "commissions, rebates, bonuses, gifts, referral fees or lead-generation fees linked to patient referrals", bans testimonials and before-and-after photos, and requires a doctor's registration number on public posts. Reported penalties escalate to 1–3 years off the register. Nobody has read the primary text, so it is unconfirmed whether the notice is binding regulation or guidance.

Routing hospital commissions through a company the doctor owns does not fix the problem. The word "indirectly" in clause 6.4 reaches payments to the doctor's own company, and the reported ban on lead-generation fees closes off the argument that the payment is a marketing fee. Hospitals have their own reason to avoid such payments, because they cannot deduct referral commissions paid to doctors for tax purposes ([ITATonline on *Peerless Hospitex*](https://itatonline.org/digest/peerless-hospitex-hospital-and-research-centre-ltd-v-pcit-2022-447-itr-60-213-dtr-81-326-ctr-249-287-taxman-711-cal-hc/)). Holding two licences doubles the exposure, since Kazakhstan's Code of Honor for medical workers also demands impartiality and independence ([Adilet](https://adilet.zan.kz/eng/docs/V2000021890)). Ranked from least to most exposed, the available structures are: a flat fee paid by the patient that is the same whichever hospital is chosen; a disclosed, fixed, fair-value fee from a hospital for services it actually receives, not linked to individual patients and with the doctor not choosing the hospital; a company the doctor does not own, with the doctor as a salaried adviser (equity held by family members still counts as indirect participation); and, most exposed, per-patient commissions paid to a company the doctor owns.

The rules in Central Asia constrain marketing as much as revenue. **Kazakhstan's main risk is advertising**: the Health Ministry says medical services may be advertised only by a holder of a state licence for the advertised medical activity, under Article 56 of the Health Code and Order ҚР ДСМ-105/2020 ([gov.kz](https://www.gov.kz/memleket/entities/dkmfk-almaty-oblast/press/news/details/1268034?lang=ru)). An unlicensed facilitator advertising "knee replacement in Delhi for $X" is exposed, and so is the Indian hospital being advertised. Clinical opinions given in Kazakhstan most likely count as licensed medical activity, which argues for running the founder's consultations through a licensed Kazakh medical entity, separate from the travel business. **Uzbekistan is stricter**: only services listed in a medical licence may be advertised, outcome guarantees are banned, fines reach 70 base calculation units ([UzDaily](https://www.uzdaily.uz/en/uzbekistan-competition-committee-urges-compliance-with-medical-advertising-rules/)), and citizens' personal data must be stored on registered servers inside the country ([Mondaq](https://mondaq.com/data-protection/1197754/the-law-on-personal-data-has-been-amended)). Kazakhstan also requires in-country storage of personal data under Law 94-V, and a May 2026 draft decree to relax this has not been confirmed as adopted ([AmCham Kazakhstan](https://amcham.kz/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/2026-05-15_AmCham_DataLocalization_Issue-Brief_ENG_v5.pdf)). Kyrgyzstan's new Digital Code is the most permissive: it allows cross-border transfer with consent, but requires registration in a registry, with fines from 2026 ([ICNL](https://www.icnl.org/post/news/kyrgyzstans-digital-code-understanding-and-complying-with-personal-data-provisions)).

India's operational rules are manageable. No facilitator licence is mandatory; NABH accreditation for medical value travel facilitators and registration on the Heal in India portal are voluntary ([NABH](https://nabh.co/programmes/medical-value-travel-facilitator-mvtf-empanelment-programme/); [KPMG](https://kpmg.com/in/en/insights/2025/07/heal-in-india-catalysing-medical-and-wellness-tourism-for-a-healthier-global-future.html)). Because the health ministry says it is "encouraging the registration and rating" of facilitators ([PIB](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/may/doc202652862301.pdf)), registering early is likely to become a competitive advantage. The e-Medical visa allows three entries and can be extended by up to 6 months on medical merit through the Foreigners Regional Registration Office ([Consulate General of India, Vladivostok](https://cgivladi.gov.in/page/advisory-for-e-visa-applicants)), and since September 2025 hospitals must report foreign patients electronically under the Immigration and Foreigners Act ([Lawrbit](https://www.lawrbit.com/article/immigration-and-foreigners-act-2025/)). On tax, intermediary services can qualify as GST-free exports since 30 March 2026 ([Grant Thornton](https://www.grantthornton.in/insights/articles/gst-on-intermediary-services/)), and the new FEMA export rules require foreign earnings to reach India within 9 months ([TaxGuru](https://taxguru.in/?p=1082965)). Foreign currency notes above $5,000 must be declared on entry ([RBI](https://rbi.org.in/SCRIPTs/BS_FemaNotifications.aspx?Id=13337)). A facilitator that collects patients' money and forwards it to hospitals takes on money-transmission, foreign-exchange and sanctions risk, so the clean design is for the hospital to invoice the patient and be paid directly. Kazakh banks already expect an invoice for transfers like these ([Finance.kz](https://finance.kz/articles/valyutnyy-kontrol-v-kazahstane)).

| Risk | Rule as reported | Exposure for this founder | Design response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Referral commissions (India) | 2002 regulations, clause 6.4 ("directly or indirectly"); the 6 Oct 2026 notice on lead-generation fees | **High**: suspension or removal from the medical register | No per-patient hospital money to any entity the doctor controls |
| The doctor's own advertising (India) | Testimonials and before-and-after photos banned; registration number required on posts (reported) | Medium | No patient testimonials in the doctor's marketing; publish data instead |
| Advertising treatment (Kazakhstan) | Allowed only with a licence for the advertised activity | **High** if Indian procedures are promoted | Advertise the licensed Kazakh consultation and the logistics; no procedure prices, no outcome claims |
| Advertising and data (Uzbekistan) | Advertising limited to licensed services; strict data localisation | **High** | Enter through a licensed Uzbek partner that holds the data |
| Clinical opinions in Kazakhstan | Probably licensed medical activity | Medium | A separate licensed medical entity |
| Transplants | Authorisation Committee approval and embassy Form 21; an Indian donor only if a near relative | **Very high (criminal)** if mishandled | Related donors only; documents go through the embassy only; never involved in finding or paying donors |
| Payments | Sanctions on Russia; cash over $5,000 must be declared | Medium to high | Wires go straight to the hospital; no money passes through the company |
| Data (India) | DPDP Act phases in until May 2027 | Medium | Master records held in Kazakhstan; transfers to India only with written consent |
| Facilitator credentials | None mandatory | Low legal risk, high commercial value | Obtain NABH facilitator accreditation and Heal in India registration early |

### A fixed-fee, physician-led patient advocate is the profitable gap

The recommended positioning is **"the family's own doctor for treatment in India"**. The founder is a Russian-speaking physician licensed in both Kazakhstan and India who **takes no money from any hospital**. The founder obtains **written, itemised quotes from three hospitals, each naming the consultant who will treat the patient**, stays on the family's side through the stay in India, and follows the patient up at home.

This positioning turns every documented failure into a feature, and turns the founder's dual licence from a compliance problem into a competitive moat. Bookimed reportedly employs 65 medical specialists ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/bookimed)), but they work remotely and none is licensed where the patient lives. No visible local agency advertises a physician, and no hospital desk is neutral.

The product works best as a defined sequence of paid stages. Every stage answers one of the gaps in the table above.

| Stage | What the family receives | Gap it closes |
|---|---|---|
| Second opinion in Kazakhstan | A paid consultation through a licensed Kazakh clinic, in person in Almaty or the south, or by video. Records are translated, and an anonymised case is sent to three accredited hospitals | Second opinions sold as a sales hook; ads that overstate what a doctor can do |
| Quote comparison | Binding quotes side by side, each naming the surgeon and stating exclusions and the rules for complications, intensive care and extra nights, in plain Russian | Quotes that change after arrival; "paid for a professor, saw a junior" |
| Contract and payment | A fixed journey fee by complexity tier; deposits wired in stages straight to the hospital; no money passes through the company | Commission conflict; carrying cash; intermediaries who disappear |
| In India | A safety-audited housing list, an interpreter-advocate who reports to the family, a daily check of the running bill against the quote, and a Telegram or WhatsApp channel for relatives. Transplant files go through the embassy's Form 21 process only | The fire, robbery and overbilling cases; relatives left uninformed |
| Back home | Discharge summary in Russian, Indian brand drugs mapped to generic and locally registered equivalents, teleconsultations with the operating surgeon, follow-up visits at the Kazakh clinic, and a written plan for complications | Post-return care that nobody verifies; distrust of Indian medicines |

The headline guarantee is about service, not outcome, which keeps it legal in both countries: *if the final bill exceeds the written quote without a documented clinical reason, the advocacy fee is refunded.*

Families will pay a fee when competitors are "free" because the free model is not free. On an $18,000 kidney transplant in India, a 20–35% facilitator commission means **$3,600–6,300 is built into the price**. A flat advocacy fee of $1,500–2,500 is cheaper, *provided the hospital charges the advocate's patients its direct-patient price*. That is the single most important commercial unknown. Before launch, the founder should use their international-desk contacts to secure **written direct-patient tariffs** from three to five NABH- or JCI-accredited hospitals. If hospitals keep the commission margin in the price whether or not a facilitator collects it, the fee is an extra cost, and the pitch must rest on safety and accountability rather than savings.

| Illustrative year-3 revenue (assumptions, not data) | Assumption | Annual revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Paid second-opinion consultations (Kazakh-licensed clinic) | 1,000 × $150 | $150,000 |
| Journey advocacy fee | 300 travelling patients × $1,800 blended (tiers of roughly $900–3,500) | $540,000 |
| Services in India (vetted housing booking, interpreter-advocate days, bill audit) | 300 × $400 | $120,000 |
| Follow-up programme after return | 250 × $250 | $62,500 |
| **Core total** | | **~$0.87 million** |
| Optional: disclosed fixed-fee services to hospitals (Russian-language desk support, translation, state-programme coordination), only if counsel approves | 3–4 contracts | +$0.1–0.2 million |

Three hundred travelling patients is roughly 1% of the identifiable Uzbek and Turkmen flow to India. For comparison, the same 300 patients at an assumed $12,000 average bill would earn **$720,000 at a 20% commission**, about the same as the advocacy model's core revenue. The clean model therefore gives up little revenue at the same volume, removes the licence risk and earns the trust that drives referrals.

Margins depend mainly on keeping the advocates in India fully used. Each one can handle only a few families at a time, so recruiting and training advocates, not marketing, is the constraint on growth. The business becomes "very profitable" only by scaling the advocate team across countries through partners, by selling its trust infrastructure (the housing registry, bill audits and translation) to other facilitators and hospitals, and by pricing the highest-acuity cases higher, where a single avoided overrun is worth many times the fee.

The rollout should follow where the founder is strongest and the regulation is lightest.

| Order | Market | Why | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Kazakhstan: Almaty plus the south (Shymkent, Turkistan, Kyzylorda) | The founder's licence, Russian-speaking patients, the highest spend, little Indian presence; the Kyzylorda case and the Yashoda agreement both point south | A licensed consultation clinic plus a separate travel company |
| 2 | Kyrgyz patients, through Almaty and Bishkek | Mostly transplants; India is far cheaper than Turkey; e-Medical visa restored; the most permissive data law | Russian-language service from Almaty; partnerships in Bishkek |
| 3 | Uzbekistan | India's largest Central Asian source, crowded with single-hospital desks, so neutrality sells | A licensed Uzbek partner holds the data and the advertising; Uzbek-speaking coordinators |
| 4 | Turkmenistan | The fifth-largest source, but no visible agencies | Through translator and diaspora networks in Delhi |
| 5 | Russia | Weak demand for surgery in India; sanctions make payments risky | Last, and only after a sanctions/AML review |

Marketing has to stay within advertising law. The licensed Kazakh clinic can advertise its own consultation service and the logistics service, but never Indian procedures, prices or outcomes. Referrals from Kazakh oncologists and nephrologists must be unpaid to stay within the Code of Honor. The strongest trust proof is data: NABH facilitator accreditation, Heal in India registration, a published table of anonymised quotes against final bills, and content built around the Ministry of Health's own August 2026 checklist.

The risks are real. "Free" is a powerful anchor for patients. Bookimed could add licensed physicians, and hospital desks could resist an advocate who audits their bills. The founder is the brand, which is hard to scale and concentrates personal liability, so errors-and-omissions insurance and a strict line between advice and treatment are essential. The conventional alternative also deserves a fair hearing: a company the doctor does not own could take hospital commissions and pay the founder a salary. It might earn more at high volume, but it gives up the neutrality claim that is the whole point, and equity held by family members can still be treated as indirect participation.

## Project 2: MBBS-abroad agents sell into a market that distrusts them

### About 25,000 students a year buy a six-year degree through an opaque chain

Demand comes from NEET arithmetic. In 2025, **12.37 lakh candidates qualified for 1.18 lakh Indian MBBS seats** ([Careers360](https://news.careers360.com/mcc-neet-ug-counselling-2025-begins-tomorrow-registration-colleges-fees-link-12-36-lakh-compete-for-1-18-lakh-mbbs-seats/amp)), and about **25,000 Indian students go abroad for MBBS each year** ([Deccan Herald/PTI](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/failing-to-get-licence-to-practice-as-doctors-3-siblings-turn-consultants-for-mbbs-aspirants-2814581)).

On 1 January 2025 the Ministry of External Affairs counted Indian university students abroad, across all disciplines but mostly in medicine: about 27,000 in Russia, 16,500 in Kyrgyzstan, 16,000 in Georgia, 9,862 in Bangladesh, 9,500 in the Philippines, 9,064 in Kazakhstan and 7,500 in Uzbekistan ([MEA](https://www.mea.gov.in/Images/CPV/557-en-01-04-12-2025.pdf)). The Uzbek number reportedly passed 10,000 later in 2025 ([The PIE News](https://thepienews.com/uzbekistan-sees-three-fold-rise-in-indian-students/)). The Kazakh cohort of about 9,000 matters most for this founder, because the founder can verify those universities, hostels and contractors in person.

The supply chain is layered and opaque by design. India's embassy in Astana says Kazakh medical universities appoint "contractors" who handle hostels, welfare "and even collecting fee". These contractors "have their sub-agents in India", and "in many cases, senior Indian students themselves function as contractors and agents" ([Embassy of India Astana](https://www.indembastana.gov.in/content/Student-advisory-Kazakhstan.pdf)). Some universities publish lists of their cooperating companies, among them Kazakh National Medical University ([Admistay](https://admistay.com/admission/kazakhstan/almaty/university/kazakh-national-medical-university-3771)). An Azim Premji University study finds that intermediaries "may not reveal all relevant information to attract more students" ([Azim Premji University](https://azimpremjiuniversity.edu.in/insights-from-social-context/indian-student-migration-for-medical-education-to-central-and-east-asia-understanding-demand-supply-and-roles-of-market-and-state)). Many consultants are foreign medical graduates themselves; PTI profiled three siblings who failed FMGE and became MBBS-abroad consultants ([Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/failing-to-get-licence-to-practice-as-doctors-3-siblings-turn-consultants-for-mbbs-aspirants-2814581)).

No MBBS-specific commission figure is public. General study-abroad benchmarks run at 10–20% of first-year tuition ([AGL](https://agl.ac/study-abroad-agent-commission-structures/)). Careers360 warns that some consultants keep part of the tuition meant for the university ([Careers360](https://studyabroad.careers360.com/articles/is-studying-mbbs-abroad-a-scam)).

The core problem is timing: agents are paid at enrolment, and outcomes arrive six years later. FMGE pass rates were about **25.8% in 2024, and 18.61% in June 2025, when 29,327 candidates failed** ([Careers360](https://news.careers360.com/fmge-result-june-2025-pass-rate-slides-18-pc-failed-29327-foreign-mbbs-graduates-difficulty-overseas-medical-education-quality)). In December 2025, 43,933 sat the exam and 10,264 passed ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/education/medical-admissions/only-10264-candidates-clear-fmge-december-2025-over-77-percent-fail-screening-test-163962)). In June 2026 only about **12.4%** passed ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/education/medicos-criticise-nbe-after-124-percent-pass-rate-in-fmge-june-2026-say-exam-was-tougher-than-neet-pg-ini-cet-174619)). Which university a student picks now matters more than which agent they use, and nothing ties an agent's income to that choice.

### Portals sell leads and consultancies sell seats, but nobody verifies agents

The big portals earn from colleges, not agents. Shiksha is described as paid by colleges per lead, and its parent says it is "pivoting its model" because AI search now answers students' queries directly ([Info Edge](https://www.infoedgeindia.com/announcements/SEDisclosure-QuarterlyUpdate_March%202026.pdf); [Medianama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/05/223-choking-traffic-ai-dents-billings-shiksha-info-edge-q4-fy26-earnings)). Collegedunia reached ₹192 crore in revenue profitably, with almost no outside capital ([Entrackr](https://entrackr.com/fintrackr/bootstrapped-collegedunia-hits-rs-200-cr-revenue-eyes-3x-growth-and-ipo-in-5-years-8960952)). It already lists consultancies ([Collegedunia](https://s3.collegedunia.com/institute/7537-abc-studyv-links-study-abroad-consultant-new-delhi)), one review alleges its leads were resold repeatedly ([MouthShut](https://www.mouthshut.com/review/collegedunia-web-pvt-ltd-review-qpnpmmnonnp)), and a reseller quotes ₹300–800 per lead for engineering and management courses, with medical leads costing more ([SmartAds](https://smartads.in/services/digital/collegedunia-advertising)). CollegeDekho shows how expensive bought student traffic is: in FY25 it spent about **₹126 crore on advertising against ₹222 crore of revenue** and lost ₹151 crore ([Entrackr](https://entrackr.com/fintrackr/collegedekho-posts-rs-151-cr-loss-in-fy25-revenue-remains-flat-11081103)). Careers360 runs the closest thing to a trust layer, with fraud warnings and blacklist explainers ([Careers360](https://studyabroad.careers360.com/articles/beware-of-fraudsters-while-planning-study-mbbs-abroad)), but it does not rate agents.

Some consultancy sites already publish FMGE pass rates by university ([RussianMBBS](https://russianmbbs.com/resources/fmge-pass-rates); [MBBSDirect](https://mbbsdirect.com/fmge-pass-rate-by-university-a-data-backed-comparison-for-russia-kyrgyzstan-uzbekistan-and-kazakhstan/)). That proves families want this data. It also shows the weakness: the sites that publish it sell admissions.

No search found a neutral, verified directory of MBBS agents. "Best consultancy" rankings are written by consultancies ([Ambitio](https://ambitio.club/blog/best-consultancy-for-mbbs-abroad/)), and business directories list firms without checking them ([Ternrise](https://ternrise.com/firms/educational-consultant-jaipur-india-9)). A model where agents pay a subscription plus a fee per contact reveal is new in this niche. The gap is not a shortage of leads; it is that nobody vouches for the agent.

Similar Indian marketplaces show both the economics and the ways such platforms lose trust. Justdial earned an average of **₹4,742 per paid campaign** in Q1 FY25 ([ICICI Direct](https://icicidirect.com/mailimages/IDirect_JustDial_CoUpdate_July2024.pdf)) but draws persistent complaints about poor leads and auto-debits that are hard to stop ([ConsumerComplaints.in](https://www.consumercomplaints.in/justdial-com-complaint-against-justdial-for-poor-lead-quality-zero-business-conversion-unresponsive-support-and-continued-ecs-deductions-d-c3543240)). IndiaMART earns about **₹67,000 a year per paying supplier** ([AlphaStreet](https://alphastreet.com/india/india-mart-q2-fy26-earnings-results)), yet lost about 1,200 net subscribers in one quarter after raising its entry-plan price ([IIFL](https://live-next.indiainfoline.com/news/markets/indiamart-structural-story-intact-despite-near-term-blip)). And when the Tamil Nadu Medical Council sent notices to doctors who ranked top in Practo's search results, asking why they had advertised ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/amp/tamil-nadu-medical-council-sends-out-more-notices-for-advertising)), it showed that paid placement which looks like a ranking invites trouble.

### Students lack the truth about eligibility, outcomes and fees

Eligibility is technical, and a mistake cannot be undone. Under the NMC's FMGL Regulations 2021, a foreign degree counts in India only if the course is at least 54 months long and taught in English, the 12-month internship is done at the same institution, and the graduate is eligible for a licence to practise in the awarding country on the same terms as its own citizens ([NMC FAQ](https://nmc.org.in/storage/cms/rules-regulation-nmc/yFQCJ2Bf9yZU5zBfLCNUFIFG2E3optNdIAQfas9Z.pdf)). Students admitted after 18 November 2021 cannot transfer ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/education/nmc-releases-faqs-based-on-queries-raised-by-foreign-medical-graduates-from-ukraine-china-97802)). The NMC also states that it "does not endorse any list" of foreign universities ([NMC](https://www.nmc.org.in/information-desk/for-students-to-study-in-abroad/)). Any "NMC-approved" badge is therefore a misrepresentation, and families must check each criterion themselves.

Destination countries change the rules mid-degree, and Kyrgyzstan is the live example. A decree of 28 August 2025 made medical education a state monopoly ([AKIpress](https://akipress.com/news:847045:President_Japarov_signs_decree_establishing_state_monopoly_on_medical_education_in_Kyrgyzstan/)). The Indian embassy said the **1 June 2026 accreditation deadline "will not be extended"** ([Embassy of India Bishkek](https://indembbishkek.gov.in/docs/1777460393ADVISORY-29.04.2026.pdf)) and named **13 institutions that failed**, mostly private universities heavily marketed to Indians ([Millennium Post](https://www.millenniumpost.in/k-reers/13-kyrgyz-medical-varsities-fail-state-accreditation-concern-for-indians-677110)). A health ministry order of 3 August 2026 transferred their students centrally ([24.kg](https://24.kg/english/384085_State_accreditation_Transfer_of_medical_school_college_students_begins/)). Because the NMC bans transfers, **more than 2,000 Indian students** do not know whether their degrees will count ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/education/kyrgystan-institutes-accreditation-row-over-2000-indian-mbbs-students-face-uncertainity-medicos-seek-mea-intervention-nmc-clarity-179915)).

In Uzbekistan, an NMC alert dated 1 April 2026 named medical institutions in Bukhara, Samarkand and Tashkent, plus a Bengaluru "offshore campus". It cited admissions beyond capacity, weak clinical training and teaching not really in English, and warned against unverified agents ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/health-news/nmc/mbbs-abroad-nmc-warns-students-against-admission-to-foreign-medical-institutes-in-uzbekistan-flags-fmgl-violations-167792)). Samarkand reportedly admitted about 1,400 Indian first-year students and housed about 600 of them in hotels ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/education/uzbekistan-medical-university-allegedly-admitting-students-beyond-intake-capacity-fmg-body-seeks-transfer-provision-from-nmc-158305)). Then in August 2026 the Indian embassy and the Haryana Medical Council called the alert precautionary, not a ban ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/education/mbbs-abroad-no-admission-ban-on-uzbekistan-medical-universities-haryana-medical-council-indian-embassy-clarify-on-nmc-advisory-176868)). Families cannot interpret mixed signals like these on their own. Elsewhere, the embassy in Astana notes that most Kazakh universities lack enough hostel space ([Embassy of India Astana](https://www.indembastana.gov.in/page/education-in-kazakhstan/)), and reports that Georgia's state universities will stop admitting foreign students in 2026–27 come only from commercial sources ([AV Global Overseas](https://www.avglobaloverseas.com/news/georgia-stops-foreign-admissions-state-universities-2026)).

Fees and fraud follow familiar patterns. Agents quote low fees, and students discover hostel, library, exam and coaching charges after they arrive ([EOCL India](https://eoclindia.com/mbbs-abroad-scams-how-to-avoid-them/)). The embassy in Tashkent tells families to pay the university directly and to get a cost breakdown from both the university and the consultant ([Embassy of India Tashkent](https://eoitashkent.gov.in/advisory-for-indian-students-june-18/)). Documented frauds include ₹10.85 lakh taken in Uttar Pradesh for a Georgian seat backed by fake documents ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/state-news/uttar-pradesh/mbbs-abroad-admission-fraud-aspirant-duped-of-rs-1085-lakh-over-georgia-seat-promise-168601)) and agents in Hyderabad booked over Kyrgyz admissions ([Hyderabad Mail](https://hyderabadmail.com/chaitanyapuri-mbbs-scam-kyrgyzstan-fake-admission-case/)). Graduates of a Bukhara institute also allege that their degrees were withheld ([The Indian Practitioner](https://theindianpractitioner.com/indian-medical-graduates-stranded-uzbek-degree-withholding/)).

The scams that do the most damage are legal at the moment of sale: an over-capacity university, a "bilingual" course, a university that later fails accreditation. Only a platform that keeps a live log of regulatory events for each university, alongside its outcome data, can catch these.

That defines the product students need. It should show FMGL compliance for each university as a checklist of the regulation's criteria rather than an "approved" badge. It should show FMGE outcomes per university from the free institution-level PDFs that NBEMS publishes, with a minimum cohort size, because the 2024 data covered 486 institutions and 31 of them posted 100% pass rates on tiny cohorts ([Careers360](https://studyabroad.careers360.com/articles/fmge-2024-abroad-mbbs-universities-100-pass-rate)). It should also keep a log of regulatory events for each university and country, give a six-year total-cost sheet with the agent's fee itemised, label each agent's tier from official contractor down to sub-agent, and accept reviews only from students with proof of enrolment.

### Paid contact reveals collide with DPDP consent and child-data rules

The DPDP Rules were notified on 13–14 November 2025 and phase in on three dates: **14 November 2025, 14 November 2026 and 14 May 2027** ([Shardul Amarchand Mangaldas](https://www.amsshardul.com/insight/enforcement-of-the-dpdp-act-and-notification-of-the-dpdp-rules/)). Once the obligations apply, consent must be specific to a purpose and must be withdrawable. A real-estate analogy captures the problem: a buyer "consented to a callback from you — not to having their data shared with 50 third parties" ([Siggnals](https://siggnals.ai/blog/dpdp-compliance-real-estate-lead-data-india)). Section 9 of the Act and Rule 10 require **verifiable parental consent for anyone under 18** and bar tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted advertising aimed at children. One source cites penalties of up to ₹200 crore ([Consently](https://www.consently.in/blog/verifiable-parental-consent-dpdp-rules-2025-edtech-gaming)). Many NEET applicants are 17, which rules out retargeting and lookalike ad campaigns aimed at minors and makes parents the natural account holders.

If the app counts as an e-commerce marketplace, the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules 2020 add obligations: a grievance officer who acknowledges complaints within 48 hours and resolves them within a month, disclosure of each agent's details, and written contracts with agents ([Khaitan & Co](https://www.khaitanco.com/thought-leaderships/Stricter-Regulations-on-E-Commerce-The-Consumer-Protection-E-Commerce-Rules-2020)). Whether a pure lead-generation app counts as a marketplace is a question for counsel.

India has no national licensing of education agents. The draft Overseas Mobility Bill 2025 creates a registry for recruitment intermediaries ([VisaHQ](https://www.visahq.com/news/2025-10-29/in/draft-overseas-mobility-facilitation-welfare-bill-2025-open-for-public-comment/)), but a parliamentary committee noted that student migration is not covered ([PRS](https://prsindia.org/policy/report-summaries/welfare-of-indian-diaspora-and-status-of-emigration-bill)). Punjab does license overseas education advisers, at fees of ₹25,000 to ₹1 lakh ([Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/141913284/)), and enforces the rule: Jalandhar cancelled 263 licences ([Tribune](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/jalandhar/licences-of-263-immigration-firms-ielts-centres-revoked-486964)). Kyrgyzstan's new state admissions centre will accredit recruiting agencies and keep a registry; an official said "any admission outside the system will be considered illegitimate" ([24.kg](https://24.kg/english/371857_Special_center_to_support_admission_of_foreign_students_in_Kyrgyzstan/); [Open.kg](https://open.kg/en/news/local-news/121217-bez-posrednikov-i-seryh-shem-kak-v-kr-menjajut-priem-inostrannyh-studentov.html)).

For the founder personally, the NMC's commission ban concerns referring *patients*. On its face it does not reach student placement, but counsel should confirm that reading.

| Rule | What it requires | What it means for the app |
|---|---|---|
| DPDP Act and Rules 2025 | Consent specific to the purpose and the recipient, which can be withdrawn; phased in until 14 May 2027 | The student consents to each named agent at the moment of reveal; a withdrawal is passed to every agent who received the contact |

### Built verification-first, the app is doable and modestly profitable

The app is **doable**, because its core data is public and official. Sources include NBEMS institution-level FMGE results ([NBEMS](https://nbe.edu.in/)), NMC alerts, embassy advisories and lists of accredited universities ([Embassy of India Astana](https://www.indembastana.gov.in/page/addendum-to-the-advisory-for-indian-students-who-wish-to-study-medicine-in-kazakhstan/)), universities' own partner lists, and soon Kyrgyzstan's agent registry. The founder can also do what no Indian portal can: inspect Kazakh universities, hostels and contractors in person.

The app is **problem-solving** only if the model is changed so that the paying side, the agents, cannot buy trust. The evidence points to six changes:

| Element of the proposed model | Recommended change | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly agent subscription | Keep entry prices low and cancellation easy; only verified agents can subscribe. Verification means company and GST registration, plus a university authorisation letter checked against the university's partner list or a state registry | IndiaMART lost subscribers after an entry-price rise; Justdial draws auto-debit complaints; embassies warn about sub-agents |
| Fee per student who views an agent's number | The student starts each reveal; each student can reveal at most 3–5 agents, and both sides are told the cap; invalid leads are refunded automatically; parent consent comes first | Avoids the resold-lead complaints seen at Justdial and Collegedunia; gives per-recipient consent under DPDP |
| Agent profiles | Show each agent's tier (official contractor, authorised representative, sub-agent) and delist anyone who asks for tuition to be paid into a personal account | The Astana embassy describes the chain of sub-agents; embassies tell families to pay universities directly |
| Ads on the app and website | Label every ad as sponsored and keep ads out of rankings; no ads from NMC-flagged or non-accredited institutions; prefer advertisers with no ranking conflict (FMGE/NExT coaching, education loans, foreign exchange, travel insurance) | The Practo precedent; adverse selection by under-enrolled universities |
| University pages | FMGE trend with cohort sizes, the FMGL checklist, the regulatory-event log and a six-year cost sheet | Scams that are legal at sale; pass rates falling to about 12% |
| Reviews | Only from students with proof of enrolment | Today's listicles are self-published |

In the base case, an agent spends about ₹3.45 lakh a year: ₹1.2 lakh in subscriptions plus 300 reveals at ₹750. If 5–10% of reveals turn into enrolments (an assumption), that works out to roughly **₹11,500–23,000 per enrolled student**. That is a small fraction of the lakh-scale sums families already hand to agents, as the ₹10.85 lakh Georgian-seat fraud shows, so agents can afford it.

The real economic risk is the cost of acquiring students. If the app buys student traffic at the market's own lead prices (₹300–800 or more), a ₹750 reveal barely covers its cost. CollegeDekho's ad-heavy losses and Shiksha's AI-search decline show where bought traffic leads. Students must instead arrive through tools they want anyway: an FMGE outcome explorer, an FMGL eligibility checker, a crisis tracker for Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan, a six-year cost calculator, YouTube explainers timed to NEET and FMGE results, and referrals from parents' groups. On that basis, the base case is a business with ₹8–12 crore of revenue that can be profitable as long as acquisition stays organic. That would be meaningful next to Careers360's total revenue of ₹46 crore ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/company/careers360/financials/)), but it is not a venture-scale outcome. Fast followers are a real threat: Shiksha is looking for new offerings to offset its search losses, and Careers360 already owns the anti-fraud content.

The positioning is **"Check before you pay"**: the only MBBS-abroad app where every agent is checked against the university's own partner list, every university shows its official FMGE record and NMC-rule compliance, and the family decides which agents get its number. Launch where the founder can verify on the ground and where the turmoil is greatest. That means verified profiles first for Kazakhstan's roughly 9,000-student market and for Kyrgyzstan, where accredited agents will soon be registered by the state and 2,000+ families are hunting for information they can trust. Russia and Uzbekistan come next.

## Conclusion

Both businesses make money the same way: they sell the customer the accountability that suppliers' agents cannot provide, and in both the founder's medical licence is the moat and the main constraint. Regulators on every side are moving towards registries. India's health ministry is pushing registration and rating of medical-travel facilitators, Kyrgyzstan is accrediting recruiting agents, and India's Overseas Mobility Bill creates a registry of intermediaries. Building verification before it is mandatory positions both businesses to become the compliant incumbent when it is. The order of work matters. Project 1's legal structure (a patient-paid fee, a licensed Kazakh consultation entity and no money from hospitals) must be settled before the first patient travels, because the commission ban reportedly tightened on 6 October 2026. Project 2's design window closes on 14 May 2027, after which per-recipient consent and parental consent have to be built in rather than retrofitted.

The two projects also share a resource that neither market uses well: the same group of people forms the informal broker class in both corridors. Indian medical graduates and senior students from Russian-speaking countries fail FMGE in their tens of thousands; there were about 63,000 failed attempts across the two 2025 sessions. Some become hospital interpreter-brokers in Delhi, and others become sub-agents recruiting the next cohort. An untested but promising hypothesis is that recruiting and credentialing some of them could supply what both projects lack. In Project 1 they would work as independent, family-paid patient advocates; in Project 2, as verified alumni reviewers. That would turn the corridor's least accountable intermediaries into its most accountable ones.

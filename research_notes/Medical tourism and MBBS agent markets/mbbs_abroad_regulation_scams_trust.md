## Q1. What decides whether a foreign medical degree is valid in India (NMC FMGL Regulations 2021, clarifications, advisories, lists, Kazakhstan 6-year programmes, CRMI internship)?

### Takeaway
A foreign MBBS counts in India only if the student was NEET-qualified and the course meets FMGL 2021 Regulation 4: at least 54 months of study in English, plus a 12-month internship at the same foreign institution, the Schedule I subjects, and registration or eligibility for a licence to practise in the country that awarded the degree, on the same terms as that country's citizens. After that come a 12-month internship in India under CRMI and the licensing exam (FMGE now, NExT later). NMC publishes no "approved list" and says it endorses none. Instead it issues institution-specific alerts; the most important recent ones are about Uzbekistan (July 2025 and 1 April 2026).

### Cited Findings
**Core FMGL 2021 eligibility (Regulation 4)**
- Regulation 4(a)(i) sets a minimum course duration of fifty-four months. NMC's own FAQ PDF cites it as "Minimum duration of fifty-four months [Reg. 4 (a) (i) of FMGL. 2021]". — [NMC FAQ PDF](https://nmc.org.in/storage/cms/rules-regulation-nmc/yFQCJ2Bf9yZU5zBfLCNUFIFG2E3optNdIAQfas9Z.pdf)
- Regulation 4(a)(iii) requires English as the medium of instruction. The same clause set also requires a 12-month internship at the same foreign institution, the mandatory subjects listed in Schedule I, and passing the National Exit Test. — [RICH GLOBAL EDU reproduction of the regulation text (secondary)](https://richglobaledu.com/fmg-2021-regulations-for-students-studying-mbbs-abroad/); [NMC FAQ PDF](https://nmc.org.in/storage/cms/rules-regulation-nmc/yFQCJ2Bf9yZU5zBfLCNUFIFG2E3optNdIAQfas9Z.pdf)
- Regulation 4(b) requires the FMG to be registered with, or otherwise competent to receive a licence from, the regulator in the country that awarded the degree, "at par with the license to practice medicine given to citizen of that country". — [NMC FAQ PDF](https://nmc.org.in/storage/cms/rules-regulation-nmc/yFQCJ2Bf9yZU5zBfLCNUFIFG2E3optNdIAQfas9Z.pdf)
- The regulations took effect on final publication in the Gazette on 18 November 2021. Under Regulation 5, students who were admitted before that date are generally covered by the earlier Screening Test regime. — [RICH GLOBAL EDU (secondary)](https://richglobaledu.com/fmg-2021-regulations-for-students-studying-mbbs-abroad/); [Medical Dialogues explainer](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/education/study-abroad/new-fmg-regulations-2021-to-change-dynamics-of-mbbs-abroad-here-is-how-84997)
- An NMC FAQ says students admitted after 18 November 2021 "must complete the entire duration of course, training and internship or clerkship in the same foreign medical institution", and that transfer is not allowed. — [Medical Dialogues on NMC FAQs for Ukraine/China FMGs](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/education/nmc-releases-faqs-based-on-queries-raised-by-foreign-medical-graduates-from-ukraine-china-97802)
- NMC FAQs are also collected on Vikaspedia (Government of India portal). — [Vikaspedia FAQs on FMGL 2021](https://education.vikaspedia.in/viewcontent/education/career-guidance/career-options/foreign-medical-graduate-licentiatie/faqs-on-fmgl-regulations-2021?lgn=en)
- Secondary sources disagree on the 10-year limit. One says the internship must be completed within 10 years of joining, another that the whole course must be finished within ten years. — [Collegedunia (secondary)](https://collegedunia.com/study-abroad/article/nmc-fmgl-regulations-20212026-7-non-negotiable-rules-for-your-foreign-degree-to-be-valid); [AMW Career Point (secondary)](https://amwcareerpoint.com/blogs/nmc-rules-mbbs-abroad-2026-fmgl-regulations-fmge-next)

### Inferences
- Regulation 4(b) on licensing at par with citizens is the clause most likely to trip up destinations where foreigners cannot get a local licence. Examples: the Philippines (see Q2), and possibly Kyrgyzstan graduates of non-accredited universities whose "diplomas will not get verified". A neutral platform should treat "graduates can get a local practice licence" as a checkable field for each university.
- Two rules together mean a university or country shock leaves students with no exit: the ban on transfers for post-Nov-2021 admissions, and the same-institution internship rule. This is the main reason Kyrgyz and Uzbek disruptions are so serious for Indian students.
- Background, unverified: the old Eligibility Certificate was effectively replaced by NEET qualification around 2018. The "EC mandatory for Kazakhstan" claim is probably outdated or wrong and should not be repeated without checking NMC's site.
- Background, unverified: CRMI 2021 requires FMGs to do a 12-month (or longer, if required) rotating internship in an NMC-recognised Indian college after passing FMGE. State medical councils allot the seats, and seats have been scarce. Not confirmed this session.

- The exact dates and full texts of the NMC notices of Aug 2023, Nov 2024 and May 2025 referred to in the April 2026 alert were not retrieved.
- I could not confirm the current status of NExT and whether FMGE is still the licensing exam for FMGs in late 2026. The June 2026 FMGE was held, so FMGE was still running in mid-2026.

## Q2. Country crises: Kyrgyzstan 2025–26 (state monopoly, 1 June 2026 accreditation, embassy advisories) and issues in Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Russia, Georgia, Tajikistan, the Philippines, Ukraine and China

### Takeaway
Kyrgyzstan is the biggest live shock. A decree of 28 Aug 2025 made medical training a state monopoly, accreditation became mandatory by 1 June 2026, and 13 institutions (mostly private ones popular with Indians) failed. Their students are being moved centrally under a Health Ministry order of 3 Aug 2026. FMGL bans transfers, so more than 2,000 Indian students are stuck until NMC clarifies. Uzbekistan has over-capacity and quality problems that NMC has flagged. Georgia's state universities are reported to have stopped new foreign admissions from 2026–27. The Philippines' BS-MD compliance is contested. Ukraine and China left a legacy of students stuck on transfers and online classes.

### Inferences
- Many of the 13 Kyrgyz institutions that failed accreditation were heavily marketed in India by agents, which suggests a large share of recent Indian cohorts are affected. The ">2,000" figure is probably a floor rather than the full count.
- The pattern across countries is the same: a destination regulator tightens rules (Kyrgyz accreditation, Georgian state-university closure, Philippine internship and licensure). FMGL's same-institution and licence-at-par rules then turn the change into an Indian eligibility problem. A trust platform should track an "event log" for each country and university.
- The Kyrgyz transfer is official in Kyrgyzstan, but it may break FMGL's same-institution rule unless NMC grants relief, as it did for the Philippines BS cases and online classes. Whether NMC will do so is the key open question as of Oct 2026.

## Q7. Marketplace abuse and legal risks for a platform that sells student leads to agents (DPDP, minors, E-Commerce Rules, IT Act)

### Takeaway
A pay-per-lead model that sells NEET students' contact data to agents carries three legal risks that need a lawyer:

1. DPDP: the Rules were notified on 13–14 Nov 2025, and most fiduciary obligations, including verifiable parental consent for under-18s, take effect on 14 May 2027. Consent must be specific to the purpose and should name the recipients. Selling one lead to many agents without that consent is likely non-compliant. Children's data also cannot be used for tracking or targeted ads, and many NEET aspirants are 17.
2. Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules 2020: a "marketplace e-commerce entity" must disclose seller (agent) details, run a grievance officer process (acknowledge within 48 hours, resolve within 1 month) and have written contracts with sellers.
3. Intermediary safe harbour: it is available only if the platform stays passive and does due diligence, and does not shield the platform from its own misleading claims, such as "verified" or "NMC approved".

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
These need a lawyer to confirm.
- **Timing.** As of 14 Oct 2026, most DPDP fiduciary duties (notice, consent, children's VPC, penalties) are reportedly not in force until 14 May 2027. A platform launched now has a design window, not an exemption. Building VPC and per-recipient consent in now avoids re-collecting the whole lead database in 2027. Also confirm which obligations start on 14 Nov 2026, one month away; reportedly the consent-manager provisions.
- **Selling one lead to many agents.** Under DPDP this probably needs consent that names, or at least clearly specifies, each agent or category of agent, for a specific purpose such as "contact me about admission to X". The consent must also be withdrawable, with the withdrawal passed on to every agent who received the lead. Bundled "by submitting you agree to be contacted by partners" consent is high risk.
- **Minors.** Many NEET takers are 17. Once Rule 10 applies, the platform needs verifiable parental consent for under-18 leads. It also cannot target ads at under-18s or track their behaviour, which affects retargeting and lookalike campaigns aimed at NEET aspirants. Collecting date of birth and putting parents first (consent from the parent as primary contact) is a practical design.
- **Agents as fiduciaries.** Agents who buy leads probably become independent data fiduciaries, with their own DPDP duties. The platform should have data-sharing contracts that bind agents to purpose limits, no resale, deletion on withdrawal and breach notification. DPDP has no processor exemption for the platform's own processing.
- **E-Commerce Rules.** If the platform lists agents and helps consumers choose them, it may count as a "marketplace e-commerce entity" for services. If so it must disclose agent identity, address and contact, run a grievance officer process, have written contracts with agents, and not manipulate rankings or present paid placement as neutral. Whether pure lead-gen (no transaction on the platform) falls under the Rules is a legal question.
- **Verification claims.** Saying "verified agent", "NMC approved university" or "guaranteed admission" may create liability under the Consumer Protection Act 2019 and the CCPA's misleading-ads and dark-patterns guidelines (background, unverified this session). This matters especially because NMC says it endorses no list.
- **IT Act section 79 safe harbour.** It probably does not cover the platform's own editorial content (ratings, badges) or active roles such as selecting or matching leads (background, unverified).
- **Abuse vectors to design against:**
  - reselling the same lead beyond the stated number of agents
  - fake or bot leads billed to agents, which is a fraud and refund issue
  - agents scraping student data and spamming
  - agents misrepresenting their university authorisations
  - fake reviews (background: India's 2022 BIS standard on online consumer reviews, IS 19000:2022, is relevant; unverified this session)
  - commission conflicts, where universities pay agents per head and agents steer students to over-capacity universities, as the SSMU and Kyrgyz cases show
